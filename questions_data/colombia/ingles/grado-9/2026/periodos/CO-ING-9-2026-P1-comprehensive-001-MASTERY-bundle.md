# Bundle MASTERY: English Period 1 – Past Perfect, Conditionals & Reported Speech

This bundle covers the first period of Grade 9 English at CEFR B1 level: past perfect tense, type 2 conditional (unreal present), and introduction to reported speech.

---

## Question 1 [D3]

**ID:** `CO-ING-9-2026-P1-comprehensive-001-MASTERY-v1`
**Bloom:** Remember
**ICFES:** Identificación de contenidos locales
**Context:** A student is reviewing past perfect tense formation.

### Enunciado
Complete the sentence with the correct past perfect form: "By the time we arrived, the movie _____."

### Options
- [ ] A) already started <!-- feedback: Incorrect. This is simple past, but the action happened before another past action. -->
- [x] B) had already started <!-- feedback: Correct. The past perfect (had + past participle) is used to show that an action was completed before another past action. "By the time we arrived" establishes the later past moment, so "had started" is the correct tense. -->
- [ ] C) has already started <!-- feedback: Incorrect. Present perfect cannot be used when the reference time is in the past. -->
- [ ] D) was already starting <!-- feedback: Incorrect. Past continuous does not express completion before another past action. -->

### Explicación Pedagógica
Past perfect (had + past participle) is used to express an action that happened before another action or time in the past. The earlier action takes past perfect; the later action takes simple past.

## Question 2 [D3]

**ID:** `CO-ING-9-2026-P1-comprehensive-001-MASTERY-v2`
**Bloom:** Remember
**ICFES:** Identificación de contenidos locales
**Context:** In class, the teacher explains the structure of type 2 conditionals.

### Enunciado
What is the correct grammatical structure for a Type 2 (second) conditional?

### Options
- [ ] A) If + present simple + will + base verb <!-- feedback: Incorrect. That's the first conditional, used for real/possible situations. -->
- [x] B) If + past simple + would + base verb <!-- feedback: Correct. The second conditional (If + past simple, would + infinitive) is used for unreal or improbable situations in the present or future. Example: "If I had more time, I would travel the world." -->
- [ ] C) If + past perfect + would have + past participle <!-- feedback: Incorrect. That's the third conditional, used for unreal past situations. -->
- [ ] D) If + present perfect + will + base verb <!-- feedback: Incorrect. This is not a standard conditional structure. -->

### Explicación Pedagógica
The second conditional expresses hypothetical, unreal, or improbable situations. The past simple in the if-clause does not refer to past time but to an unreal present situation.

## Question 3 [D4]

**ID:** `CO-ING-9-2026-P1-comprehensive-001-MASTERY-v3`
**Bloom:** Understand
**ICFES:** Identificación de contenidos locales
**Context:** A student needs to transform direct speech into reported speech.

### Enunciado
If someone says "I am tired," how do you report this in the past?

### Options
- [ ] A) She said she is tired <!-- feedback: Incorrect. When the reporting verb is in the past, the verb tense usually shifts back. -->
- [ ] B) She said she was being tired <!-- feedback: Incorrect. "Was being" is past continuous, which doesn't fit here. -->
- [x] C) She said she was tired <!-- feedback: Correct. In reported speech, when the reporting verb is past (said), the present tense "am" shifts to past "was." This is called backshift of tenses. -->
- [ ] D) She said she has been tired <!-- feedback: Incorrect. Present perfect is not the correct backshift for present simple. -->

### Explicación Pedagógica
In reported speech, when the reporting verb is in the past tense (said, told, asked), verbs shift back: present → past, past → past perfect, etc. "I am" → "she was."

## Question 4 [D4]

**ID:** `CO-ING-9-2026-P1-comprehensive-001-MASTERY-v4`
**Bloom:** Understand
**ICFES:** Comprensión global
**Context:** Students analyze the difference between first and second conditionals.

### Enunciado
What is the difference between "If it rains, I will stay home" (1st conditional) and "If it rained, I would stay home" (2nd conditional)?

### Options
- [ ] A) There is no difference; they mean the same thing <!-- feedback: Incorrect. They express different levels of probability. -->
- [ ] B) The first is about the past; the second is about the future <!-- feedback: Incorrect. The first conditional is about the future. -->
- [x] C) The first conditional expresses a real possibility (it might rain), while the second conditional expresses an unreal or improbable situation (I don't expect it to rain) <!-- feedback: Correct. The first conditional (if + present, will) is used for real, possible situations. The second conditional (if + past, would) is for hypothetical or unlikely situations. The past tense in the second conditional signals unreality, not past time. -->
- [ ] D) The first is polite; the second is impolite <!-- feedback: Incorrect. Politeness is not the grammatical distinction here. -->

### Explicación Pedagógica
The choice of conditional structure reflects how probable the speaker considers the condition. The second conditional often expresses dreams, wishes, or situations contrary to current reality: "If I won the lottery, I would buy a house."

## Question 5 [D5]

**ID:** `CO-ING-9-2026-P1-comprehensive-001-MASTERY-v5`
**Bloom:** Apply
**ICFES:** Identificación de estructuras
**Context:** A student writes a paragraph using past perfect and simple past correctly.

### Enunciado
Choose the correct option: "When I _____ (arrive) at the party, Maria _____ (leave) already."

### Options
- [ ] A) arrived / left <!-- feedback: Incorrect. Both in simple past doesn't clarify which happened first. -->
- [ ] B) had arrived / had left <!-- feedback: Incorrect. Both in past perfect is unnecessary. Only the earlier action needs past perfect. -->
- [x] C) arrived / had left <!-- feedback: Correct. The arrival (arrived) is in simple past, and Maria's departure (had left) happened before that, so it takes past perfect. This clearly shows the sequence: first Maria left, then I arrived. -->
- [ ] D) arrive / has left <!-- feedback: Incorrect. Present tenses are not appropriate for a past context. -->

### Explicación Pedagógica
Past perfect establishes the order of two past events. The earlier event takes past perfect; the later event takes simple past. This avoids ambiguity about which event happened first.

## Question 6 [D5]

**ID:** `CO-ING-9-2026-P1-comprehensive-001-MASTERY-v6`
**Bloom:** Apply
**ICFES:** Identificación de estructuras
**Context:** Students practice transforming direct questions to reported speech.

### Enunciado
How do you correctly report this question: "Do you like coffee?" she asked.

### Options
- [ ] A) She asked if do I like coffee <!-- feedback: Incorrect. Reported questions do not use auxiliary "do" in the same way. -->
- [ ] B) She asked if I liked coffee <!-- feedback: Correct. In reported yes/no questions, use "if" or "whether." The verb "like" shifts back (liked), and the word order changes from question to statement (I liked, not do I like). -->
- [ ] C) She asked if I like coffee <!-- feedback: Incorrect. Without backshift, it's not fully correct reported speech when the reporting verb is past. -->
- [ ] D) She asked that I like coffee <!-- feedback: Incorrect. "That" is not used to introduce reported yes/no questions. Use "if" or "whether." -->

### Explicación Pedagógica
Reporting questions requires: 1) changing question word order to statement word order, 2) backshifting the tense, 3) using "if"/"whether" for yes/no questions, and 4) removing the question mark.

## Question 7 [D5]

**ID:** `CO-ING-9-2026-P1-comprehensive-001-MASTERY-v7`
**Bloom:** Apply
**ICFES:** Comprensión global
**Context:** A student must transform a direct command into reported speech.

### Enunciado
"Clean your room!" my mom told me. In reported speech:

### Options
- [ ] A) My mom told me that clean my room <!-- feedback: Incorrect. The structure is missing "to." -->
- [x] B) My mom told me to clean my room <!-- feedback: Correct. Reported commands use tell + person + to + infinitive. The imperative "clean" becomes "to clean." -->
- [ ] C) My mom told me that I cleaned my room <!-- feedback: Incorrect. This is a statement, not a reported command. -->
- [ ] D) My mom told me cleaning my room <!-- feedback: Incorrect. The gerund is not used for reported commands. -->

### Explicación Pedagógica
Reporting commands: tell + object + to + infinitive. Example: "Sit down" → He told me to sit down. For negative commands: "Don't run" → He told me not to run.

## Question 8 [D6]

**ID:** `CO-ING-9-2026-P1-comprehensive-001-MASTERY-v8`
**Bloom:** Analyze
**ICFES:** Comprensión global
**Context:** Students read a short paragraph and must identify all examples of past perfect.

**Text: "After she had finished her homework, she watched TV. She had already eaten dinner, so she wasn't hungry. Her brother hadn't arrived yet."**

### Enunciado
How many past perfect verbs are in the text?

### Options
- [ ] A) One <!-- feedback: Incorrect. There are more. -->
- [x] B) Three — "had finished", "had eaten", "hadn't arrived" <!-- feedback: Correct. All three verbs use "had + past participle": had finished, had eaten, hadn't arrived. Each indicates an action completed before another past action or time. -->
- [ ] C) Two — only "had finished" and "had eaten" <!-- feedback: Incorrect. "Hadn't arrived" is also past perfect (negative form). -->
- [ ] D) Four <!-- feedback: Incorrect. "Watched" and "was" are simple past. -->

### Explicación Pedagógica
Past perfect can be affirmative (had + past participle), negative (hadn't + past participle), and interrogative (had + subject + past participle). It always refers to the "past of a past."

## Question 9 [D6]

**ID:** `CO-ING-9-2026-P1-comprehensive-001-MASTERY-v9`
**Bloom:** Analyze
**ICFES:** Identificación de estructuras
**Context:** Students analyze the meaning of a second conditional sentence.

### Enunciado
"If I were you, I would apologize." What does this sentence imply?

### Options
- [ ] A) The person might apologize later <!-- feedback: Incorrect. The second conditional implies unreality. -->
- [x] B) The speaker is giving advice, but the listener is not the speaker. The "were" shows this is an unreal situation because the speaker cannot be the listener <!-- feedback: Correct. "If I were you" (using "were" for all persons, not "was") is a fixed expression for giving advice. It implies: "I can't be you, but if it were possible, my recommendation would be to apologize." The subjunctive "were" signals unreality. -->
- [ ] C) The person will definitely apologize <!-- feedback: Incorrect. The second conditional expresses hypothetical situations. -->
- [ ] D) The speaker wants to become the listener <!-- feedback: Incorrect. It's a rhetorical device for giving advice, not a literal wish. -->

### Explicación Pedagógica
"Were" in the second conditional is the subjunctive form. Use "were" for all subjects (I, he, she, it) in unreal conditionals: "If she were taller, she would play basketball" (not "was").

## Question 10 [D6]

**ID:** `CO-ING-9-2026-P1-comprehensive-001-MASTERY-v10`
**Bloom:** Analyze
**ICFES:** Identificación de estructuras
**Context:** Students analyze reported speech transformations for time expressions.

### Enunciado
She said, "I will call you tomorrow." Which reported speech version is correct?

### Options
- [ ] A) She said she will call me tomorrow <!-- feedback: Incorrect. Without backshift, this is direct speech punctuation removed, not true reported speech. -->
- [ ] B) She said she would call me the next day <!-- feedback: Correct. In reported speech, "will" becomes "would" and "tomorrow" becomes "the next day" or "the following day." This reflects both tense backshift and time expression shift. -->
- [ ] C) She said she would call me tomorrow <!-- feedback: Partially correct. The tense backshift is correct (will → would), but "tomorrow" should shift to "the next day" for formal reported speech, although it's common in informal contexts. -->
- [ ] D) She said she called me the next day <!-- feedback: Incorrect. "Called" changes the meaning entirely (past action, not future intention). -->

### Explicación Pedagógica
In reported speech, time expressions also shift: now → then, today → that day, yesterday → the day before, tomorrow → the next day, here → there.

## Question 11 [D7]

**ID:** `CO-ING-9-2026-P1-comprehensive-001-MASTERY-v11`
**Bloom:** Analyze
**ICFES:** Comprensión global
**Context:** Students read a paragraph mixing past perfect and simple past.

**Text: "She had never flown before, so she was nervous. Her friend had booked a window seat for her. The pilot announced that they would depart in 10 minutes."**

### Enunciado
Why does the text use past perfect in the first two sentences but simple past in the third?

### Options
- [ ] A) The author made a mistake <!-- feedback: Incorrect. The tense choice is intentional. -->
- [x] B) Past perfect is used for background information (experiences before the main narrative moment), while simple past advances the narrative (the announcement happened at the narrative time) <!-- feedback: Correct. Past perfect sets the background: her previous experience (had never flown) and actions completed before (had booked). Simple past moves the story forward (announced). This is a typical narrative pattern: past perfect for background, simple past for main events. -->
- [ ] C) Simple past is always wrong in narratives <!-- feedback: Incorrect. Simple past is the default tense for narrative. -->
- [ ] D) Past perfect should be used everywhere in past narratives <!-- feedback: Incorrect. Overusing past perfect makes the text awkward. It's used only to clarify sequence. -->

### Explicación Pedagógica
In narrative writing, past perfect provides background information or "earlier past" actions. Simple past advances the main storyline. This alternation creates clarity about the sequence of events.

## Question 13 [D8]

**ID:** `CO-ING-9-2026-P1-comprehensive-001-MASTERY-v13`
**Bloom:** Analyze
**ICFES:** Reflexión crítica
**Context:** Students compare direct and reported speech in a dialogue.

**Direct: "I didn't see Carlos yesterday," Maria said.**
**Reported: Maria said she hadn't seen Carlos the day before.**

### Enunciado
What grammatical transformations occurred in this reported speech example?

### Options
- [ ] A) Only the pronoun changed (I → she) <!-- feedback: Incorrect. Multiple changes occurred. -->
- [x] B) Three changes: pronoun (I → she), verb tense shift (didn't see → hadn't seen / past → past perfect), and time expression (yesterday → the day before) <!-- feedback: Correct. 1) Pronoun shifts: I → she. 2) Backshift: simple past "didn't see" → past perfect "hadn't seen." 3) Deixis shift: "yesterday" (relative to speaker's now) → "the day before" (relative to reporting moment). -->
- [ ] C) Only the verb changed <!-- feedback: Incorrect. Pronouns and time expressions also change. -->
- [ ] D) No changes are needed because the tense is already past <!-- feedback: Incorrect. Simple past shifts to past perfect in reported speech. -->

### Explicación Pedagógica
Reported speech requires multiple simultaneous adjustments: person (pronouns), tense (backshift), time/place references (deixis), and word order (for questions). Each change depends on the context and the reporting verb tense.

## Question 14 [D8]

**ID:** `CO-ING-9-2026-P1-comprehensive-001-MASTERY-v14`
**Bloom:** Evaluate
**ICFES:** Reflexión crítica
**Context:** A student evaluates whether the past perfect is necessary in a given sentence.

### Enunciado
Which sentence is better and why? "When I arrived, the train left" vs. "When I arrived, the train had left."

### Options
- [ ] A) Both are equally correct and mean the same thing <!-- feedback: Incorrect. They imply different sequences. -->
- [ ] B) The first sentence is better because it is simpler <!-- feedback: Incorrect. Simpler is not always clearer or more correct. -->

Using past perfect when it's needed avoids ambiguity. Good writers use it to clarify the sequence of events. In speech, context often clarifies, but in writing, the past perfect is essential for precision.

## Question 15 [D9]

**ID:** `CO-ING-9-2026-P1-comprehensive-001-MASTERY-v15`
**Bloom:** Evaluate
**ICFES:** Reflexión crítica
**Context:** Students evaluate the use of conditionals in argumentative writing.

### Enunciado
A student writes: "If the government would invest more in education, the country develops faster." What is wrong with this sentence, and how should it be corrected?

### Options
- [ ] A) It is perfectly correct <!-- feedback: Incorrect. There is a grammatical error in the conditional structure. -->
- [ ] B) It should be: "If the government invests more in education, the country will develop faster" (first conditional for real possibility) <!-- feedback: This is a correct form but changing the meaning to a real conditional. Let me see the other options. -->
- [x] C) It should be: "If the government invested more in education, the country would develop faster" (second conditional for hypothetical situation). The original incorrectly mixes "would" in the if-clause and uses present simple in the result clause. <!-- feedback: Correct. The original has two errors: 1) using "would" in the if-clause (except for polite requests), and 2) using present simple in the result clause. The standard second conditional is: If + past simple (invested), would + base verb (would develop). -->
- [ ] D) It should be: "If the government had invested more in education, the country would develop faster" <!-- feedback: Incorrect. This is a mixed conditional (third + second) that implies the investment didn't happen in the past. -->

### Explicación Pedagógica
Common mistakes with conditionals: putting "would" in the if-clause (✗ If I would have time), or mixing structures inconsistently. Remember: "would" only goes in the result clause, not the if-clause.
