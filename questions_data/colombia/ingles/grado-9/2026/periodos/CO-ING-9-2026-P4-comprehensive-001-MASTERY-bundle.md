# Bundle MASTERY: English Period 4 – Relative Clauses, Wish/Prefer & Intermediate Phrasal Verbs

This bundle covers the fourth period of Grade 9 English at CEFR B1 level: defining and non-defining relative clauses, expressions with wish and prefer, and intermediate phrasal verbs.

---

## Question 1 [D3]

**ID:** `CO-ING-9-2026-P4-comprehensive-001-MASTERY-v1`
**Bloom:** Remember
**ICFES:** Identificación de contenidos locales
**Context:** The teacher introduces relative clauses.

### Enunciado
What is the function of a relative clause?

### Options
- [ ] A) To express a completed action in the past <!-- feedback: Incorrect. That's the function of past tenses. -->
- [ ] B) To give additional information about a noun without starting a new sentence <!-- feedback: Correct. A relative clause functions as an adjective: it modifies a noun. "The student who won the contest is my friend." The clause "who won the contest" gives information about "the student." Relative clauses are introduced by relative pronouns (who, which, that, whose, where, when). -->
- [ ] C) To ask a question about a person or thing <!-- feedback: Incorrect. That's the function of interrogative clauses. -->
- [ ] D) To express a hypothetical situation <!-- feedback: Incorrect. That's the function of conditional clauses. -->

### Explicación Pedagógica
Relative clauses allow us to combine two sentences into one, making writing more cohesive. "I have a friend. She lives in Medellín." → "I have a friend who lives in Medellín."

## Question 2 [D3]

**ID:** `CO-ING-9-2026-P4-comprehensive-001-MASTERY-v2`
**Bloom:** Remember
**ICFES:** Identificación de contenidos locales
**Context:** Students learn the structure of "I wish" sentences.

### Enunciado
What is the correct structure after "I wish" to express a desire for a different present situation?

### Options
- [ ] A) I wish + present simple <!-- feedback: Incorrect. "I wish I study more" is not correct. -->
- [ ] B) I wish + will + verb <!-- feedback: Incorrect. "Wish" is not followed by "will" in standard grammar (except "I wish you would" for complaints). -->
- [x] C) I wish + past simple (or past continuous) <!-- feedback: Correct. To express a wish about the present, use I wish + past simple (or past continuous). "I wish I studied more" = I don't study enough. "I wish I were taller" = I am not tall. The past tense signals unreality. -->
- [ ] D) I wish + past perfect <!-- feedback: Incorrect. Past perfect is used for wishes about the past, not the present. -->

### Explicación Pedagógica
"Wish" expresses a desire for something different from reality. Present wish: wish + past simple (I wish I had more money = I don't have enough money). Past wish: wish + past perfect (I wish I had studied = I didn't study).

## Question 3 [D4]

**ID:** `CO-ING-9-2026-P4-comprehensive-001-MASTERY-v3`
**Bloom:** Understand
**ICFES:** Identificación de contenidos locales
**Context:** Students learn the difference between defining and non-defining relative clauses.

### Enunciado
What is the difference between a defining (restrictive) and a non-defining (non-restrictive) relative clause?

### Options
- [ ] A) There is no difference; they are the same <!-- feedback: Incorrect. They have different functions and punctuation. -->
- [x] B) A defining clause is essential to identify the noun (no commas), while a non-defining clause adds extra information that could be removed without changing the meaning (uses commas) <!-- feedback: Correct. Defining: "The students who studied hard passed" (only those students). Non-defining: "The students, who studied hard, passed" (all students studied hard). Non-defining clauses are set off by commas and cannot use "that." -->
- [ ] C) Defining clauses use "which" and non-defining use "who" <!-- feedback: Incorrect. Both types can use various pronouns. -->
- [ ] D) Non-defining clauses are always at the end of sentences <!-- feedback: Incorrect. They can appear in the middle too. -->

### Explicación Pedagógica
Punctuation is key: non-defining clauses have commas; defining clauses don't. The meaning changes based on which type you use. "My brother who lives in Bogotá is a doctor" (I have more than one brother) vs. "My brother, who lives in Bogotá, is a doctor" (I have one brother).

## Question 4 [D4]

**ID:** `CO-ING-9-2026-P4-comprehensive-001-MASTERY-v4`
**Bloom:** Understand
**ICFES:** Comprensión global
**Context:** Students practice expressing preferences with "prefer."

### Enunciado
Which sentence correctly uses "prefer" to express a general preference?

### Options
- [ ] A) I prefer coffee that tea <!-- feedback: Incorrect. The comparison structure is wrong. -->
- [ ] B) I prefer coffee than tea <!-- feedback: Incorrect. Use "to" or "over" for comparisons, not "than." -->
- [x] C) I prefer coffee to tea <!-- feedback: Correct. "Prefer + noun + to + noun" is the correct comparative structure. Example: "I prefer reading to watching TV." "Prefer + gerund + to + gerund" is also correct. -->
- [ ] D) I prefer more coffee than tea <!-- feedback: Incorrect. "Prefer" already implies "like more," so "more" is redundant. -->

### Explicación Pedagógica
"Prefer" patterns: prefer + noun + to + noun (I prefer dogs to cats), prefer + gerund + to + gerund (I prefer swimming to running), prefer + to + infinitive + rather than + base verb (I prefer to walk rather than drive).

## Question 5 [D5]

**ID:** `CO-ING-9-2026-P4-comprehensive-001-MASTERY-v5`
**Bloom:** Apply
**ICFES:** Identificación de estructuras
**Context:** Students combine two sentences using a relative pronoun.

### Enunciado
Combine: "The girl won the competition. She is my cousin."

### Options
- [ ] A) The girl won the competition who is my cousin <!-- feedback: Incorrect. The relative clause should immediately follow the noun it modifies. -->
- [x] B) The girl who won the competition is my cousin <!-- feedback: Correct. The relative clause "who won the competition" directly follows "the girl" and identifies which girl we are talking about. "Who" is used because the antecedent is a person. -->
- [ ] C) The girl is my cousin who won the competition <!-- feedback: Incorrect. This could imply the cousin won the competition, but it's awkward. The relative clause should be close to the noun it modifies. -->
- [ ] D) The girl which won the competition is my cousin <!-- feedback: Incorrect. "Which" is used for things and animals, not for people. Use "who" for people. -->

### Explicación Pedagógica
Relative pronouns: who (people), which (things/animals), that (people or things, in defining clauses only), whose (possession), where (places), when (times). Place the relative clause as close as possible to the noun it modifies.

**ID:** `CO-ING-9-2026-P4-comprehensive-001-MASTERY-v6`
**Bloom:** Apply
**ICFES:** Identificación de estructuras
**Context:** Students practice "wish" for present situations.

### Enunciado
Complete: "I don't speak English fluently. I wish I _____ English fluently."

### Options
- [ ] A) speak <!-- feedback: Incorrect. Present simple after wish doesn't express unreality. -->
- [ ] B) would speak <!-- feedback: Incorrect. "Would" after wish is for complaints about someone's behavior, not for personal desires. -->
- [x] C) spoke <!-- feedback: Correct. To express a wish about the present that is contrary to reality (I don't speak fluently), use wish + past simple (spoke). "I wish I spoke English fluently" = I don't speak fluently, but I want to. -->
- [ ] D) had spoken <!-- feedback: Incorrect. Past perfect after wish is for past regrets, not present desires. -->

### Explicación Pedagógica
Wish + past simple = present unreal. Wish + past perfect = past regret. Wish + would = complaint about someone. Example: "I wish you wouldn't interrupt me" (complaint).

**Bloom:** Apply
**ICFES:** Comprensión global
**Context:** Students practice intermediate phrasal verbs in context.

### Enunciado
What does the phrasal verb "break down" mean in this sentence? "My car broke down on the highway."

### Options
- [ ] A) My car exploded <!-- feedback: Too extreme. "Break down" doesn't mean explode. -->
- [x] B) My car stopped functioning / stopped working <!-- feedback: Correct. "Break down" means a vehicle or machine stops functioning. It can also mean to lose emotional control ("She broke down crying") or to analyze something in detail ("Let's break down the problem"). -->

- [ ] D) My car crashed <!-- feedback: Incorrect. "Crashed" means hit something, not mechanical failure. -->

### Explicación Pedagógica
"Break down" is an inseparable phrasal verb. It has multiple meanings: 1) stop working (machine/vehicle), 2) lose emotional control (person), 3) analyze/divide into parts (information). Context determines the meaning.

## Question 8 [D6]

**ID:** `CO-ING-9-2026-P4-comprehensive-001-MASTERY-v8`
**Bloom:** Analyze
**ICFES:** Comprensión global
**Context:** Students analyze relative clause usage in a paragraph.

**Text: "My aunt, who lives in Cali, is a teacher. The school where she works has a program that helps students from rural areas. The students, who come from different towns, receive full scholarships."**

### Enunciado
Which relative clauses in this text are non-defining (non-restrictive)?

### Options
- [ ] A) "that helps students from rural areas" <!-- feedback: Incorrect. That defines which program, and it has no commas. -->
- [x] B) "who lives in Cali" and "who come from different towns" — both have commas and provide extra information <!-- feedback: Correct. "who lives in Cali" is non-defining (we already know which aunt — my only aunt). "who come from different towns" is non-defining (all students in this program come from different towns). Both have commas and could be removed without changing basic meaning. -->
- [ ] C) "where she works" <!-- feedback: Incorrect. This is a defining clause. -->
- [ ] D) All are non-defining <!-- feedback: Incorrect. "That helps students" and "where she works" are defining. -->

### Explicación Pedagógica
Non-defining clauses: 1) always set off by commas, 2) cannot use "that" (use who/which), 3) provide extra, not essential, information. Defining clauses: no commas, can use "that," essential to identify the noun.

## Question 9 [D6]

**ID:** `CO-ING-9-2026-P4-comprehensive-001-MASTERY-v9`
**Bloom:** Analyze
**ICFES:** Identificación de estructuras
**Context:** Students analyze the difference between wish + past and wish + would.

### Enunciado
What is the difference between "I wish it stopped raining" and "I wish it would stop raining"?

### Options
- [ ] A) They mean exactly the same thing <!-- feedback: Incorrect. There's a subtle difference. -->
- [ ] B) The first is about the present; the second is about the past <!-- feedback: Incorrect. Both express desire about the present/future. -->
- [x] C) "I wish it stopped raining" expresses a desire for a different present reality (a wish about a state). "I wish it would stop raining" expresses impatience or complaint about the rain's continuation (wish + would expresses annoyance about something outside our control) <!-- feedback: Correct. Wish + past simple is a general desire for a different present. Wish + would expresses frustration with a situation that the subject has control over (or in this case, nature that won't change). "Wish + would" often implies "I'm tired of this / this is annoying." -->
- [ ] D) The first is grammatically incorrect <!-- feedback: Incorrect. Both are correct. -->

### Explicación Pedagógica
"Wish + would" is typically used when: 1) the subject of would is different from the subject of wish (I wish you would...), 2) it expresses complaint or impatience, 3) the situation is controllable. We don't say "I wish I would..." for personal desires.

## Question 10 [D6]

**ID:** `CO-ING-9-2026-P4-comprehensive-001-MASTERY-v10`
**Bloom:** Analyze
**ICFES:** Identificación de estructuras
**Context:** Students classify phrasal verbs as separable or inseparable.

### Enunciado
Which phrasal verb is inseparable (the object must go after the particle)?

### Options
- [ ] A) Turn off <!-- feedback: Incorrect. "Turn off" is separable. "Turn off the TV" = "Turn the TV off." -->
- [ ] B) Pick up <!-- feedback: Incorrect. "Pick up" is separable. "Pick up the book" = "Pick the book up." -->
- [x] C) Look after <!-- feedback: Correct. "Look after" is inseparable. The object always goes after "after": "Look after the children" (NOT "Look the children after"). Common inseparable phrasal verbs: look after, care for, run into, go over, get along with. -->
- [ ] D) Put on <!-- feedback: Incorrect. "Put on" is separable. "Put on your shoes" = "Put your shoes on." -->

### Explicación Pedagógica
Some phrasal verbs are inseparable: the verb and particle must stay together. The object always goes after the particle. Common intermediate inseparables: run into (meet by chance), get along with (have a good relationship), go through (experience), look forward to (anticipate).

## Question 11 [D7]

**ID:** `CO-ING-9-2026-P4-comprehensive-001-MASTERY-v11`
**Bloom:** Analyze
**ICFES:** Comprensión global
**Context:** Students analyze the meaning of "wish" in different contexts.

### Enunciado
"I wish I had studied more for the test." What does this sentence imply?

### Options
- [ ] A) The speaker is currently studying <!-- feedback: Incorrect. "Had studied" is past perfect, referring to the past. -->
- [ ] B) The speaker doesn't care about the test <!-- feedback: Incorrect. The wish shows the speaker does care, in retrospect. -->
- [x] C) The speaker did NOT study enough, and the test result was probably negative. This is a past regret expressed with wish + past perfect <!-- feedback: Correct. The past perfect after wish (had studied) refers to an unreal past: the studying didn't happen, and now the speaker regrets it. The implication is that the test result was poor because of insufficient preparation. -->
- [ ] D) The speaker studied a lot and got a good grade <!-- feedback: Incorrect. The wish expresses regret about what didn't happen. -->

### Explicación Pedagógica
Wish + past perfect always expresses regret about the past. The opposite of reality is implied: "I wish I had arrived earlier" = I didn't arrive earlier and I regret it. "If only" can be used similarly: "If only I had studied more."

## Question 12 [D7]

**ID:** `CO-ING-9-2026-P4-comprehensive-001-MASTERY-v12`
**Bloom:** Apply
**ICFES:** Comprensión global
**Context:** Students practice using relative clauses with "whose" for possession.

### Enunciado
Combine: "I have a friend. His father is a famous artist."

### Options
- [ ] A) I have a friend who his father is a famous artist <!-- feedback: Incorrect. "Who his" is not grammatical. -->
- [ ] B) I have a friend that his father is a famous artist <!-- feedback: Incorrect. Same problem: "that his" is not correct. -->
- [x] C) I have a friend whose father is a famous artist <!-- feedback: Correct. "Whose" is the possessive relative pronoun. It replaces "his father" → "whose father." It works for both people and things. "The car whose engine failed" is also correct. -->
- [ ] D) I have a friend of which the father is a famous artist <!-- feedback: Incorrect. "Of which" is used for things, not people, and sounds very formal. -->

### Explicación Pedagógica
"Whose" is the possessive form of who. It can refer to people (a friend whose car) and things (a book whose cover). It is the only correct way to express possession in relative clauses.

## Question 13 [D8]

**ID:** `CO-ING-9-2026-P4-comprehensive-001-MASTERY-v13`
**Bloom:** Analyze
**ICFES:** Reflexión crítica
**Context:** Students analyze the use of relative clauses in formal vs. informal English.

### Enunciado
In informal English, which relative pronoun is often omitted (zero relative clause)?

### Options
- [ ] A) "Who" when referring to the subject <!-- feedback: Incorrect. Subject relative pronouns cannot be omitted. -->
- [x] B) "That" or "whom" when it is the object of the clause <!-- feedback: Correct. Object relative pronouns can be omitted in informal English. Formal: "The book that I read" → Informal: "The book I read." If the relative pronoun is the subject of the clause, it cannot be removed: "The book that won the prize" (that = subject, cannot be omitted). Object pronouns: "The movie (that) we saw" (that = object of saw). -->
- [ ] C) "Which" can always be omitted <!-- feedback: Incorrect. "Which" as subject cannot be omitted. -->
- [ ] D) No relative pronouns can ever be omitted <!-- feedback: Incorrect. Object relative pronouns are frequently omitted in spoken English. -->

### Explicación Pedagógica
Subject vs. object relative pronoun omission: SUBJECT: "The person who called you" (who = subject of called) → cannot omit "who." OBJECT: "The person (who) you called" (who = object of called) → can omit "who." This is called a contact clause.

Which sentence is correct English? "I prefer to walk rather than take the bus" vs. "I prefer walking to taking the bus."

- [ ] B) Only the second is correct <!-- feedback: Incorrect. Both are correct. -->

## Question 15 [D9]

**ID:** `CO-ING-9-2026-P4-comprehensive-001-MASTERY-v15`
**Bloom:** Evaluate
**ICFES:** Reflexión crítica
**Context:** A student evaluates an email with phrasal verb errors and must correct them.

**Email: "I came up a problem with my project. I need to look into the solution. Can you help me figure it?"**

### Enunciado
What phrasal verb errors does this email contain?

### Options
- [ ] A) No errors; the email is perfect <!-- feedback: Incorrect. There are errors. -->
- [ ] B) "Look into" is wrong; it should be "look at" <!-- feedback: Incorrect. "Look into" (investigate) is used correctly here. -->
- [x] C) "Came up" is incomplete — it should be "came up with" (to think of or encounter). "Figure it" should be "figure it out" (to understand/solve). "Came up" without "with" means moved upward, which doesn't fit <!-- feedback: Correct. "Come up WITH" = think of/encounter (inseparable: come up with a problem). "Come up" alone = move upward. "Figure out" = understand/solve (separable: figure it out). The email is missing the particles "with" and "out." These are intermediate-level phrasal verb errors. -->
- [ ] D) "Came up" should be "came across" <!-- feedback: Partially correct. "Come across" (find by chance) would also work, but the main error is missing particle. -->

### Explicación Pedagógica
Omitting the particle from a phrasal verb changes or destroys the meaning. "Come up" ≠ "come up with." "Figure" ≠ "figure out." With separable phrasal verbs, when using a pronoun, it MUST go between verb and particle: "figure it out" (NOT "figure out it").
