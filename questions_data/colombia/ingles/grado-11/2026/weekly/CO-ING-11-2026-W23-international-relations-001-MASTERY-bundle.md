### Enunciado
A commentary on international relations in Manizales contrasts two headlines: 'Foreign minister visits the border' and 'Border visit by the foreign minister.' What does the nominalization in the second version change for the reader?

### Opciones
- [x] D) It treats the event as an established fact rather than something happening now.
  <!-- feedback: 'Border visit' is a noun phrase, so the event is presented as a completed and identifiable thing instead of a live action. The reader receives the visit as a settled fact, which is what the nominal style does to agency and timing. -->
- [ ] A) It makes the event sound as though it has only just begun.
  <!-- feedback: A noun phrase carries no progressive meaning, so it cannot suggest an action under way. If anything the nominal form removes the sense of something in progress, which is the opposite of what this option claims. -->
- [ ] B) It identifies who is responsible for the visit.
  <!-- feedback: The agent is stated in both versions: 'the foreign minister' appears as the subject in the first and inside the noun phrase in the second. Nominalization shifts emphasis onto the event; it does not add or remove the agent. -->
- [ ] C) It removes every trace of the visit from the sentence.
  <!-- feedback: The visit is named directly in 'border visit', so it remains fully present in the headline. What changes is the grammatical status of the words, not whether the event is mentioned at all. -->

### Explicacion Pedagogica
Nominalization converts an action into a noun, and in doing so it recasts a process as an object: static, datable and open to compound modification. In reporting and diplomacy this routinely hardens events into facts, which is why C1+ readers benefit from recognising the nominal form and asking who has been made the subject of the sentence.

A negotiation log on international relations in Cartagena records: 'The committee reached agreement after three days, which produced the framework everyone signed.' Where is the agent of the producing?

### Opciones
- [x] A) It is suppressed, because 'which produced' follows the agreement as the subject.
  <!-- feedback: 'Agreement' is the grammatical subject of 'produced', so the framework is presented as arising from an abstraction rather than from anyone's deliberate act. A nominalization of this kind quietly removes a doer from the sentence. -->
- [ ] B) It is the committee, named in the first clause and carried over by the relative pronoun.
  <!-- feedback: 'Which' refers back to the event of reaching agreement, not to the committee, and in any case the committee is not the subject of 'produced'. The subject is the thing that came out of it, the framework. -->
- [ ] C) It is the framework, which produces the agreement in the reading the sentence gives.
  <!-- feedback: The direction is the other way round: the agreement produced the framework. Reading the framework as the producer inverts the causality that 'which produced' states plainly. -->
- [ ] D) It is made explicit by 'everyone', which names the people who signed.
  <!-- feedback: 'Everyone' identifies who signed, which is a different role from producing the framework. Naming the signatories does not supply an agent for the clause the sentence is asking about. -->

### Explicacion Pedagogica
When a process is nominalized and then made the subject of a following verb, the human agent often disappears: 'the agreement produced the framework' makes an abstraction act. Politicians and officials rely on this shift routinely, and C1+ readers trained to ask 'who did this?' will notice immediately when the answer is a noun rather than a person.

## Question 13 [D7-D8]
**ID:** CO-ING-11-2026-W23-international-relations-001-MASTERY-bundle-v13
**Bloom:** Analyze

**Contexto:** Discussing international relations in Cali.

### Enunciado
Complete the following sentence about international relations: 'Talks continued for eleven weeks, and their __________ only became clear once the mediators published the draft annex.' (Topic: a nominal expression built on an -ion noun)

### Opciones
- [x] C) outcome
  <!-- feedback: 'Talks' is a process, so what the sentence needs to name is the thing that came out of it. 'Their outcome' is the nominal expression that lets the process act as a possessed thing, which is the structure being tested. -->
- [ ] A) explaining
  <!-- feedback: A gerund noun cannot follow 'their' in that way, and 'their explaining' would name the act of explaining rather than the result of the talks. The sentence is about what the talks produced, not what they consisted of. -->
- [ ] B) circumstance
  <!-- feedback: 'Circumstance' names a condition, and the eleven weeks of talking are not a condition that belongs to the talks. What belongs to them as the result of a process is an outcome, not the circumstances they ran in. -->
- [ ] D) reasons
  <!-- feedback: 'Their reasons' would name why the talks happened or were held, which is not what became clear afterwards. The clause reports a result emerging from the process, so the noun has to express that result. -->

### Explicacion Pedagogica
A verbal process such as 'talks' can be given a derived noun and then treated as the possessor of another noun: 'their outcome'. This double layer is what nominalization looks like in practice, and choosing the right derived noun is a matter of asking whether the sentence refers to the process itself, its motivation or its result.

## Question 16 [D7-D8]
**ID:** CO-ING-11-2026-W23-international-relations-001-MASTERY-bundle-v16
**Bloom:** Analyze
**ICFES:** Structural Cloze
**Expected_Success:** 0.37
**Contexto:** Discussing international relations in Cali.

### Enunciado
Complete the following sentence about international relations: 'On balance the corridor reopening had been expected, but the schedule slipped for reasons the ministry never __________ into the record.' (Topic: a phrasal verb whose particle is obligatory)

### Opciones
- [x] D) explained
  <!-- feedback: 'Explain something into existence' is not idiomatic: 'into' cannot serve as the particle of 'explain'. The record itself already exists, and what the ministry failed to do was account for the delay in it. -->
- [ ] A) accounted
  <!-- feedback: 'Account for' is the correct phrasal verb here, since accounting for a delay means setting out the reasons for it. Using 'accounted' alone would drop the particle and lose the sense of explanation the sentence needs. -->
- [ ] B) looked
  <!-- feedback: 'Look into' is a licensed verb, but what the ministry never did was account for the reasons; the following 'the record' needs the verb that puts something on the record. Looking would not fit the phrase at all. -->
- [ ] C) put
  <!-- feedback: 'Put the record' reverses the direction of the relationship: the ministry would be placing the record somewhere rather than committing a reason to it. The preposition 'into' also requires a motion towards an interior, not a record. -->

### Explicacion Pedagogica
Part of the phrasal verb and the preposition are not interchangeable: 'account for', 'look into' and 'put on' each select a different complement, and choosing the wrong one breaks the meaning even when the sentence parses. C1+ students are expected to know the fixed particle as part of the verb rather than treating it as optional decoration.
