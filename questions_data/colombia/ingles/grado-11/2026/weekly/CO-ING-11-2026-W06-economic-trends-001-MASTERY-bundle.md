### Enunciado
Complete the following sentence about economic trends: 'Seldom __________ a country's export figures revised so late without the finance ministry first warning that the earlier series had been misclassified.' (Topic: inversion after 'seldom')

### Opciones
- [ ] A) is
  <!-- feedback: A singular auxiliary would not agree with the plural subject 'a country's export figures'. Since the figures, not the revision, are being revised, the inversion has to begin with 'are'. -->

## Question 16 [D7-D8]

**ICFES:** Structural Cloze
**Expected_Success:** 0.37
**Contexto:** Discussing economic trends in Cartagena.

### Enunciado
Complete the following sentence about economic trends: 'Rarely __________ a central bank both cut rates and praise the move in the same statement, since doing so would signal that the previous cuts had been too timid.' (Topic: inversion with 'rarely' in the present simple)

### Opciones
- [x] A) does
  <!-- feedback: Correct. 'Rarely' at the start of the clause forces inversion; the sentence describes a general habit in the present simple, so the auxiliary 'does' goes before the subject and the main verbs stay in the base form: 'rarely does a central bank cut and praise'. -->
- [ ] B) has
  <!-- feedback: Incorrect. 'Has' would make a present perfect, which needs past participles: 'rarely has a central bank cut rates and praised the move'. Here the second verb is the base form 'praise', so the clause is present simple and needs 'does'. -->
- [ ] C) did
  <!-- feedback: 'Did' belongs to the past simple, not the present perfect, and it would also require the base form of the verb. The sentence is about a recurring habit in current statements, which is the present simple under inversion. -->
- [ ] D) will

After a negative adverbial such as 'rarely', English inverts the auxiliary and the subject. In compound tenses the existing auxiliary moves instead: 'rarely has a bank praised', 'rarely will a bank praise'.

### Explicacion Pedagogica
'Make ends meet' combined with 'barely' or 'hardly' describes serious financial pressure. Understanding the adverb is as important as understanding the idiom, because it changes the degree of the difficulty.
