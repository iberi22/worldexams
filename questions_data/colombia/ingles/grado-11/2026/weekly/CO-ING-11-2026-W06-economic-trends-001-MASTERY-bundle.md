### Opciones

### Enunciado
A commentary on economic trends in Manizales observes: 'Coffee prices having fallen for three straight quarters, exporters are now unable to plan beyond a single harvest.' What does the opening participle clause do?

### Opciones
- [x] A) It presents the fall in prices as the established background against which exporters now operate.
  <!-- feedback: 'Having fallen' is a perfect participle clause that places the price drop entirely before the main clause begins. The word 'now' then marks the consequence: the situation has already happened and the exporters are living with it. -->
- [ ] B) It says that exporters caused the fall in coffee prices.
  <!-- feedback: A participle clause carries no agent, and 'having fallen' has a non-human subject, the prices themselves. No exporter is named as the cause, so this reading reverses the logic of the sentence. -->
- [ ] C) It makes clear that exporters expect prices to rise again soon.
  <!-- feedback: The sentence is about a difficulty, not an expectation of recovery. A participle clause states something that has already happened; it cannot by itself signal what will happen in the future. -->
- [ ] D) It shows that the three quarters were counted before the price data existed.
  <!-- feedback: 'Having fallen' relates the falling to a prior time, not the counting to the data. The phrase 'for three straight quarters' measures the duration of the fall, and nothing in the sentence raises any question about when the figures were produced. -->

### Explicacion Pedagogica
A perfect participle clause fronted at the start of a sentence supplies background the reader must hold in mind for the main clause. At C1+ the key check is temporal: 'having + past participle' places the action strictly before the main clause, and 'now' confirms the main clause follows from it.

**Contexto:** Discussing economic trends in Manizales.

### Enunciado
Complete the following sentence about economic trends: 'Retail sales having fallen for two quarters, __________ the case for a further cut in rates.' (Topic: a dangling participle whose subject is the preceding clause)

### Opciones
- [x] B) strengthened
  <!-- feedback: When a clause begins with a noun phrase followed by 'having', the main verb belongs to that subject. 'Retail sales' are the subject here, and a fall in them strengthens rather than weakens the argument for cutting rates. -->
- [ ] A) weakened
  <!-- feedback: 'Weakened' would contradict the reasoning set up by the opening. Falling retail sales are precisely the evidence for a rate cut, so they make the case stronger rather than weaker. -->
- [ ] C) complicated
  <!-- feedback: 'Complicated' implies the case became harder to make, and the two quarters of decline produce a straightforward evidential link. Nothing in the sentence suggests the reasoning has been obscured. -->
- [ ] D) repeated
  <!-- feedback: 'Repeated' would treat the main clause as a bare noun phrase with no verb, and the sentence requires a full verb after the dangling participle. The two quarters are reported in the opening, not restated by the main clause. -->

### Explicacion Pedagogica
A clause opening with 'having' takes over the subject of the main verb, which is the source of the error this item targets. At C1+ the reliable move is to ask who the subject is at that moment, because the answer determines the meaning of the main verb more than any sense the verb carries on its own.

## Question 12 [D7-D8]
**ID:** CO-ING-11-2026-W06-economic-trends-001-MASTERY-bundle-v12
**Bloom:** Analyze
**ICFES:** Structural Cloze
**Expected_Success:** 0.49
**Contexto:** Discussing economic trends in Cartagena.

### Enunciado
Complete the following sentence about economic trends: 'Seldom __________ a country's export figures revised so late without the finance ministry first warning that the earlier series had been misclassified.' (Topic: inversion after 'seldom')

### Opciones
- [x] D) are
  <!-- feedback: 'Seldom' is a negative frequency adverbial, and the main clause must be inverted: the plural auxiliary 'are' moves in front of the subject, giving 'seldom are a country's export figures revised'. The figures are the thing being revised, so a plural auxiliary is required. -->
- [ ] A) is
  <!-- feedback: A singular auxiliary would not agree with the plural subject 'a country's export figures'. Since the figures, not the revision, are being revised, the inversion has to begin with 'are'. -->
- [ ] B) have
  <!-- feedback: 'Have' would form a perfect, and a perfect cannot be inverted on its own in this pattern; it would need 'have the figures been', which the sentence does not allow. The construction here is a plain passive in the plural. -->
- [ ] C) being
  <!-- feedback: A gerund cannot serve as the inverted auxiliary that follows 'seldom'. The verb of the passive must be the finite 'are revised', so 'being' leaves the clause without a main verb. -->

### Explicacion Pedagogica
Inversion with negative frequency adverbs such as 'seldom', 'rarely' and 'never' is the same mechanism as with 'hardly', but it applies to the present too. C1+ students must first decide the tense and the number from the subject, then place the auxiliary before it.

### Enunciado
Complete the following sentence about economic trends: 'Sooner __________ the statistical office release the revised series than have economists debate last quarter's growth rate on the old numbers.' (Topic: comparative inversion with 'sooner...than')

### Opciones
- [x] D) had
  <!-- feedback: 'Sooner...than' forms a comparative structure, and 'than have' carries the auxiliary forward by inversion: 'sooner had the statistical office released the revised series than have economists debated'. The past perfect is what the two actions require. -->
- [ ] A) has
  <!-- feedback: A present perfect would mean the office has not yet released the series, and the comparative 'sooner...than' would then have nothing to measure against. The sentence assumes the release and the debate are both completed events. -->
- [ ] B) does
  <!-- feedback: 'Does' belongs to the present simple and would leave the past perfect 'have released' with no past auxiliary in front of the subject. The two halves of the comparison need matching past forms, which is what 'had' supplies. -->
- [ ] C) will
  <!-- feedback: 'Will' places the release in the future, but the whole sentence weighs two past events against each other. A future auxiliary in the first half would destroy the comparison with 'than have economists debated'. -->

### Explicacion Pedagogica
Comparative inversion with 'sooner...than' is a C1 pattern in which the auxiliary of the second half travels back to the first. Students should read the structure as a single tense shared by both actions, and recognise that the first half receives the auxiliary that the second half displays.

  <!-- feedback: 'Rarely' at the start of a main clause forces inversion, and with the present perfect that means the auxiliary 'has' contracts to 'does' before a base verb: 'rarely does a central bank cut and praise'. The tense of the auxiliary is what changes, not the main verb. -->

## Question 20 [D9-D10]

**Bloom:** Evaluate
**ICFES:** Pragmatic Competence
**Expected_Success:** 0.25
**Contexto:** Discussing economic trends in Cartagena.

### Enunciado
In a high-level debate about economic trends, Alejandro says: 'Headline growth was four per cent and I would put that number nowhere, because the base it was measured from has been revised twice.' What is Alejandro doing?

### Opciones
- [x] D) He is dismissing a published statistic on the grounds that it rests on a base that has since changed.
  <!-- feedback: 'I would put that number nowhere' rejects the figure, and the clause after 'because' supplies his reason: the comparison base has been revised. He is not disputing the arithmetic but the basis on which it was calculated. -->
- [ ] A) He is confirming that growth was stronger than reported.
  <!-- feedback: If anything the reported figure is described as flattering, since it rests on a base that was revised downward. Nothing in the sentence supports the official number rather than undercutting it. -->
- [ ] B) He is proposing a new method for measuring growth.
  <!-- feedback: No method is suggested anywhere in the quotation. He criticises the base used, which is a criticism of the published calculation rather than a proposal for a replacement. -->
- [ ] C) He is quoting the statistics office directly.
  <!-- feedback: The four per cent figure is reported by Alejandro himself and then rejected. Nothing in the sentence attributes the number to an institution or repeats a source's wording. -->

### Explicacion Pedagogica
This item tests a C1+ pattern in which a speaker cites a figure in order to reject it, which is a common move in panel debate. At C1+ students should note the reporting clause and the hedge before the number, since they mark the citation as something being handled rather than quoted.
