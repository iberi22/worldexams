# Bundle Mastery: Conditionals Type 2 and 3 - Grado 7
## Semana W03 — Unreal present and past conditionals

## Pregunta 1 [D3]
**ID:** `CO-ENG-7-2026-W03-conditional-type-2-3-001-MASTERY-v1`
**Bloom:** Remember
**ICFES:** Uso comprensivo del conocimiento científico
**Context:** In English class at Colegio Anglo Colombiano in Bogotá, the teacher introduces the second conditional.
**Expected_Success:** 0.85

### Enunciado
What structure does the second conditional use?

### Opciones
- [ ] A) If + present simple, will + infinitive <!-- feedback: Incorrect. That's the first conditional. -->
- [x] B) If + past simple, would + infinitive <!-- feedback: Correct. The second conditional uses past simple and would. -->
- [ ] C) If + past perfect, would have + past participle <!-- feedback: Incorrect. That's the third conditional. -->
- [ ] D) If + present simple, present simple <!-- feedback: Incorrect. That's the zero conditional. -->

### Explicacion Pedagogica
The second conditional (also called "unreal conditional") is used for hypothetical or unlikely situations in the present or future. The structure is: If + past simple, would + base verb. Example: "If I had a million pesos, I would travel around the world." Note that the past tense does NOT refer to past time here; it expresses unreality.

**ID:** `CO-ENG-7-2026-W03-conditional-type-2-3-001-MASTERY-v2`

**ICFES:** Uso comprensivo del conocimiento científico
**Context:** María is studying the third conditional for her exam at school in Medellín.
**Expected_Success:** 0.83

### Enunciado
When do we use the third conditional?

### Opciones
- [ ] A) To talk about real facts and general truths. <!-- feedback: Incorrect. That's the zero conditional. -->
- [ ] B) To talk about possible future situations. <!-- feedback: Incorrect. That's the first conditional. -->
- [ ] C) To talk about hypothetical present situations. <!-- feedback: Incorrect. That's the second conditional. -->
- [x] D) To talk about unreal situations in the past and imagine different outcomes. <!-- feedback: Correct. The third conditional expresses regret or hypothetical past. -->

### Explicacion Pedagogica
The third conditional expresses impossible situations in the past — things that didn't happen. Structure: If + past perfect, would have + past participle. Example: "If I had studied harder, I would have passed the exam." (But I didn't study hard, and I didn't pass.) It is often used to express regret, criticism, or relief about past events.

**ID:** `CO-ENG-7-2026-W03-conditional-type-2-3-001-MASTERY-v3`
**Bloom:** Understand
**ICFES:** Explicación de fenómenos
**Context:** Carlos says: "If I were the president, I would improve education." His classmate in Cali asks what this means.
**Expected_Success:** 0.80

### Enunciado
What does Carlos's sentence imply?

### Opciones
- [ ] A) He is going to be president. <!-- feedback: Incorrect. The second conditional expresses an unlikely situation. -->
- [ ] B) He was president in the past. <!-- feedback: Incorrect. Past tense in second conditional does not mean past time. -->
- [x] C) He is not the president, so this is a hypothetical/impossible situation in the present. <!-- feedback: Correct. "If I were" shows unreality. -->
- [ ] D) He will be president in the future. <!-- feedback: Incorrect. -->

### Explicacion Pedagogica
The second conditional uses past tense to talk about present/future situations that are unreal, unlikely, or imaginary. "If I were the president" tells us that the speaker is NOT the president. Note that we use "were" for all subjects (I, he, she, it) in second conditional, not "was" — this is called the subjunctive mood.

## Pregunta 5 [D5]
**ID:** `CO-ENG-7-2026-W03-conditional-type-2-3-001-MASTERY-v5`
**Bloom:** Apply
**ICFES:** Indagación
**Context:** Andrés is writing a letter to his friend in Barranquilla. He wants to say: "If I had money, I would buy a new phone."
**Expected_Success:** 0.75

### Enunciado
Is Andrés's sentence a second conditional? Why?

### Opciones
- [ ] A) No, because "had" is past tense. <!-- feedback: Incorrect. Second conditional uses past tense. -->
- [ ] B) Yes, because it talks about a real situation. <!-- feedback: Incorrect. It's hypothetical. -->
- [x] C) Yes, because it uses past simple (had) + would + infinitive (buy) to express an unreal present situation. <!-- feedback: Correct. The structure matches the second conditional. -->
- [ ] D) No, because he doesn't say "if I were." <!-- feedback: Incorrect. "If I had" is also correct for second conditional. -->

### Explicacion Pedagogica
The sentence "If I had money, I would buy a new phone" is a correct second conditional. It means: I don't have money now (unreal present), so I can't buy a phone. The structure is: If + past simple (had), would + base verb (buy). "If I were" is used with adjectives or nouns ("If I were rich"), but "if I had" is correct for possession.

Which sentence correctly expresses regret about not studying?

### Opciones
- [ ] A) If I study, I would pass the exam. <!-- feedback: Incorrect. Mixed structure. -->
- [ ] B) If I studied, I would pass the exam. <!-- feedback: This is second conditional, refers to present/future, not past regret. -->
- [x] C) If I had studied, I would have passed the exam. <!-- feedback: Correct. Third conditional expresses past regret. -->

To express regret about a past action (or inaction), we use the third conditional. "If I had studied" (but I didn't), "I would have passed" (but I didn't). This sentence expresses the speaker's regret about not studying. The second conditional ("If I studied") would be about a hypothetical present situation, not a past one.

**ID:** `CO-ENG-7-2026-W03-conditional-type-2-3-001-MASTERY-v7`
**Bloom:** Analyze
**ICFES:** Indagación
**Context:** Valentina is comparing two sentences: S1: "If it rains, I will stay home." S2: "If it rained, I would stay home." She asks about the difference.
**Expected_Success:** 0.68

### Enunciado
What is the difference between S1 (first conditional) and S2 (second conditional)?

### Opciones
- [ ] A) S1 is about the past; S2 is about the present. <!-- feedback: Incorrect. -->
- [ ] B) There is no difference in meaning. <!-- feedback: Incorrect. The meaning is different. -->
- [x] C) S1 expresses a real possibility (it might rain); S2 expresses an unlikely or imaginary situation. <!-- feedback: Correct. First conditional is possible; second is hypothetical. -->

The choice of conditional changes the likelihood the speaker assigns to the condition.

- [ ] A) If I was you, I would study more. <!-- feedback: Incorrect. "If I were you" is the correct form. -->
- [x] B) If I were you, I would study more. <!-- feedback: Correct. "If I were" is the subjunctive form. -->

This is common when a past action (or inaction) has a present result.

**Context:** Daniel is editing a text and finds: "If she would have studied, she would have passed." He thinks it might be wrong.
**Expected_Success:** 0.48

### Enunciado
Is "If she would have studied, she would have passed" correct?

### Opciones
- [ ] A) Yes, it is perfectly correct. <!-- feedback: Incorrect. It's a common error. -->
- [ ] B) No, but it's acceptable in formal writing. <!-- feedback: Incorrect. It's usually considered non-standard. -->

A common error is putting "would" in the if-clause. The correct structure is: If + past perfect (had studied), would have + past participle (would have passed). "Would" should only appear in the result clause, not the if-clause. This is a grammar rule for all conditional sentences.

**Context:** In class at the school in Sincelejo, the teacher asks: "Can we use 'could' or 'might' instead of 'would' in conditionals?"
**Expected_Success:** 0.42

### Enunciado
Which sentence correctly uses a modal other than "would" in a conditional?

### Opciones
- [ ] A) If I had more time, I could help you. <!-- feedback: Correct. "Could" can replace "would" in second conditional. -->
- [ ] B) If I have money, I could buy it. <!-- feedback: Incorrect. First conditional uses "will" or modals with present tense. -->

- [x] D) Both A and C are correct. <!-- feedback: Incorrect. Only A is correct. -->

### Explicacion Pedagogica
In conditional sentences, we can use "could" (ability/possibility) or "might" (probability) instead of "would" in the result clause. For second conditional: "If I had more time, I could help you" (ability) or "If I saved money, I might buy a car" (possibility). For third conditional: "If I had studied, I could have passed" or "I might have passed."
