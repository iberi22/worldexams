## Questions 1-6: Passage about the Grand Canyon

The Grand Canyon, carved by the Colorado River over millions of years, offers visitors an unparalleled glimpse into Earth's geological past. Located in Arizona, the canyon stretches 277 miles in length and reaches depths of over a mile. Each layer of exposed rock tells a story spanning nearly two billion years.

Geologists estimate that the Colorado River began carving the canyon approximately five to six million years ago. [1] This relatively recent timeframe, however, does not diminish the awe-inspiring scale of the result. [2] The canyon's unique ecosystem supports over 1,500 plant species and 355 bird species. [3] Visitors from around the world come to witness its colorful rock layers and dramatic vistas. [4]

The National Park Service estimates that approximately six million people visit the Grand Canyon each year. [1] This popularity has created both benefits and challenges for the park. [2] Tourism generates significant revenue for local communities; however, it also strains natural resources and disrupts wildlife. [3] The park has implemented various sustainability initiatives to balance visitor access with environmental protection. [4]

## Questions 7-12: Passage about American Inventors

Thomas Edison, often called the "Wizard of Menlo Park," held over 1,000 patents during his lifetime. His inventions, including the phonograph and the motion picture camera, transformed daily life for millions of Americans. However, Edison's legacy extends beyond his individual achievements. He pioneered a new model of innovation: the industrial research laboratory.

Edison's Menlo Park laboratory employed dozens of scientists and engineers who worked collaboratively on multiple projects simultaneously. [1] This approach, revolutionary for its time, allowed for rapid prototyping and refinement of new technologies. [2] The model was soon copied by companies like General Electric and AT&T. [3] Modern tech giants like Apple and Google continue to draw inspiration from this approach. [4]

Edison's work was not without controversy. [1] He developed the electric chair as a method of capital punishment, arguing that it was more humane than hanging. [2] His laboratories also experimented on animals, drawing criticism from animal rights advocates. [3] These aspects of his legacy complicate the narrative of pure scientific progress. [4]
