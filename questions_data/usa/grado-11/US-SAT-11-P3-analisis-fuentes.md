## Questions 1-8: Dual Passages

**Passage A: "The Case for Cryptocurrency" (Economics)**

Bitcoin and other cryptocurrencies represent a fundamental shift in how we conceptualize money. Unlike traditional currencies issued by governments, cryptocurrencies operate on decentralized networks using blockchain technology—a transparent, tamper-proof ledger accessible to anyone. Proponents argue this eliminates the need for intermediaries like banks, reducing transaction fees and increasing financial inclusion for the 1.7 billion unbanked adults worldwide.

In El Salvador, Bitcoin became legal tender in 2021. President Nayib Bukele cited increased remittances—money sent home by Salvadorans working abroad—as a primary justification. Prior to legalization, families paid an average of $40 in fees per $300 remittance. With Bitcoin, advocates claim these fees drop to near zero. However, critics point out that cryptocurrency volatility makes it impractical for daily transactions: Bitcoin's value swung from $69,000 in November 2021 to under $17,000 by late 2022.

**Passage B: "Cryptocurrency's Hidden Costs" (Environmental Science)**

The environmental impact of cryptocurrency mining has drawn increasing scrutiny. Bitcoin's proof-of-work consensus mechanism requires specialized computers solving complex mathematical problems—a process consuming approximately 150 terawatt-hours of electricity annually, comparable to the entire country of Argentina. In Texas, where electricity is cheap and regulations are minimal, cryptocurrency mining operations have strained the state's power grid, contributing to blackouts during the 2022 heat wave.

Defenders of cryptocurrency argue that renewable energy will eventually power most mining operations. While some facilities in Iceland and Canada do use geothermal and hydroelectric power, the majority of global mining remains dependent on fossil fuels. A 2022 study in the journal Joule found that for every dollar of Bitcoin value created, the cryptocurrency generates $0.49 in climate damage—a ratio that exceeds even aviation fuel emissions.

## Questions 9-16: Dual Passages on Artificial Intelligence

**Passage A: "Artificial Intelligence in the Classroom" (Education)**

The integration of AI tools like ChatGPT into American classrooms presents both opportunities and challenges. In the Los Angeles Unified School District, some teachers have embraced AI as a personalized tutoring companion, noting that struggling students who previously hesitated to ask questions now engage freely with chatbots. "It never judges, never tires, and can explain concepts twenty different ways until understanding clicks," says math teacher Roberto Hernandez.

However, concerns about academic integrity have followed. The College Board, which administers the SAT, has updated its testing protocols to detect AI-generated essays. In response to cheating concerns, some universities—including the University of Michigan—now require proctored examinations for all written assessments. Critics argue this represents an expensive arms race that diverts resources from actual learning.

**Passage B: "The AI Writing Debate" (Technology)**

The question of whether AI-generated text constitutes genuine thinking has sparked fierce philosophical debate. Dr. Patricia Walls, a cognitive scientist at MIT, argues: "Language models don't understand meaning—they predict patterns. A chatbot describing grief sounds like grief, but there's no actual emotional experience behind it." This view suggests that using AI for writing assignments is fundamentally dishonest.

On the other side, philosopher Dr. James Oduya of Stanford counters: "Humans have always used tools to augment thinking—from the abacus to spell-check. AI is simply the next iteration. The question isn't whether AI writes, but how it changes what it means to be a writer." This perspective implies that adapting to AI is not cheating but evolution.
