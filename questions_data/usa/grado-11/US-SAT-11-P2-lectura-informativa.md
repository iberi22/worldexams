**Passage: "The Rise of Urban Beekeeping" (Science Article)**

In 2019, New York City legalized beekeeping, joining dozens of major metropolitan areas that have embraced urban apiaries. What was once considered a rural practice has migrated to rooftops, balconies, and community gardens across Manhattan, Brooklyn, and Queens. Advocates argue that urban bees are crucial for pollinating city gardens and reducing food miles, while critics raise concerns about public safety and colony collapse.

Research from the University of Michigan suggests that urban hives actually produce more honey per colony than their rural counterparts, likely due to reduced pesticide exposure and diverse flowering plants in city environments. However, Dr. Elena Vasquez, an entomologist at Cornell University, cautions that urban beekeeping is not without challenges: "City bees face unique stressors including heat reflection from buildings, limited foraging ranges, and exposure to air pollution."

The New York City Beekeepers Association reports that over 400 registered hives now operate within city limits, with numbers growing 15% annually since legalization.

**Passage: "The Forgotten Frontier: Solar Energy in the American Southwest" (History/Social Studies)**

In 1973, architect Edward L. R. D. "Ed"Mazria began experimenting with passive solar design in Santa Fe, New Mexico. His goal was simple: design buildings that captured winter sunlight for heating while using strategic overhangs to provide shade in summer. The results were groundbreaking—a home in Los Alamos achieved 80% of its heating from the sun alone, using no mechanical systems whatsoever.

Despite these early successes, solar energy remained largely marginal throughout the 1970s and 1980s. The oil crises of the era prompted temporary interest, but cheap fossil fuels quickly reasserted their dominance. "The industry was always one price shock away from relevance," notes energy historian Dr. Maria Santos of Arizona State University. "When oil dropped to $10 per barrel in 1986, thousands of solar contractors went out of business overnight."

Only in the past decade has solar experienced a true renaissance, driven by plummeting panel costs and federal tax incentives. Arizona now generates more solar energy per capita than any other state, while California's ambitious mandate requires all new homes to include solar systems starting 2020.

## Passages for Questions 13-18

**Passage: "The Social Media Paradox: Connection or Isolation?" (Psychology/Sociology)**

A landmark study published in the Journal of Social and Clinical Psychology examined the relationship between social media use and mental health among 143 college students in Oregon. Participants were divided into two groups: one limited to 30 minutes daily on Instagram, Facebook, or TikTok; the other continued normal usage. After three weeks, the limited-use group reported significant reductions in loneliness and depression compared to the control group.

Lead researcher Dr. Melissa C. Holloway explains: "We found that passive scrolling—simply viewing others' content without interacting—was most strongly associated with negative outcomes. Active engagement, such as messaging friends directly, showed neutral or even positive effects."

However, some experts urge caution in interpreting these results. Dr. James Okafor of Stanford University argues: "Three weeks is far too short to draw meaningful conclusions about mental health impacts. We need longitudinal studies tracking users over years, not weeks." He also notes that correlation does not imply causation—people struggling with depression may simply be more drawn to passive social media use.
